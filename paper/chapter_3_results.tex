\chapter{Results}

\section{Introduction}

From the simulations described in chapter 2, the mean magnetization $\langle M(T) \rangle$ as a function of the temperature $T$ can be extracted for every lattice size $N$ and for every dimension $d$. As discussed in chapter 1, the magnetization of a single configuration is computed as:

\begin{equation}
	M(T) = \frac{1}{N^d} \left| \sum_i s_i \right|
\end{equation}

where $s_i$ is the spin of the $i$-th site of the lattice, and the absolute value is taken to avoid cancellation of positive and negative values in the sum. $\langle M(T) \rangle$ is then computed as the mean of the magnetization of all the $n$ configurations in the Markov Chain at a given temperature $T$.

The configurations remaining in the Markov Chain after removing the thermalisation steps and the filtering discussed in chapter 2 are supposed to be independent samples from the Boltzmann distribution. This is, in practice, not exactly true near the critical temperature, as discussed; therefore, just to be sure, the error associated with the mean magnetization is the one for the mean of correlated samples, which is computed as:

\begin{equation}
	\hat{\sigma}_{\langle M(T) \rangle} =\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{M(T)}}{\sqrt{n}} \sqrt{2 \tau_{\text{int}}} 
\end{equation}

where if the samples are truly independent (as in most cases), $\tau_{\text{int}} = \frac{1}{2}$ and the error reduces to the standard error of the mean.

Note that since $M$ is not really relevant if not to compute $\langle M \rangle$, for ease of notation the mean magnetization will be denoted as $M$ in the rest of the paper.

\br

After processing the data generated by the simulations to obtain the magnetizations, the critical parameter $T_C$ can be estimated.

As stated in chapter 1, while the drop in the magnetization curve suggests the temperature point at which the phase transition occurs, the numerically robust method to estimate this parameter is to look at $\chi$. In the following sections this method will be put into practice in two different ways.

The most simple and direct method to estimate the critical temperature is to take the maximum of $\chi$, where $\chi$ is computed directly from the magnetization data using the result of the fluctuation-dissipation theorem, which give this relation between the susceptibility and the variance of the magnetization as a function of temperature:

\begin{equation}
	\hat{\chi} = \frac{N^d}{T} \left( \langle M^2 \rangle - \langle |M| \rangle^2 \right)
\end{equation}

where the last term is the square of the average of the absolute value of the magnetization, which is used to avoid the cancellation of positive and negative values in the average.

Errors on the values of $\chi$ are computed with the standard error propagation formula, which gives:

\begin{equation}
	\hat{\sigma}_{\hat{\chi}} = \frac{N^d}{T} \sqrt{ \sigma_{\langle M^2 \rangle}^2 + 4 \langle \vert{}M\vert{} \rangle^2 \sigma_{\langle \vert{}M\vert{} \rangle}^2 - 4 \langle \vert{}M\vert{} \rangle \text{Cov}(\langle M^2 \rangle, \langle \vert{}M\vert{} \rangle) }
\end{equation}

Where:

\begin{itemize}
	\item $\sigma_{\langle M^2 \rangle}$ is the error on the mean of the square of the magnetization.
	\item $\sigma_{\langle \vert{}M\vert{} \rangle}$ is the error on the mean of the absolute value of the magnetization.
	\item $\langle \vert{}M\vert{} \rangle$ is the mean of the absolute value of the magnetization.
	\item $\text{Cov}(\langle M^2 \rangle, \langle \vert{}M\vert{} \rangle)$ is the covariance of the means.
\end{itemize}

Given the discrete nature of the data in the temperature space, in order to better estimate the maximum of $\chi$ its maximum sampled value and the two nearest data points will be interpolated with a cubic spline. The maximum of the interpolated function will be taken as the estimate of the critical temperature $T_C$.

For a quick estimation of the error on $\hat{T_C}$ one can consider the Taylor expansion of $\chi(T)$ around the maximum point $\hat{T}_\text{max}$ and evaluate it at the point $\hat{T_C}$:

\begin{equation}
	\chi(T)\big|_{T_C} \approx \chi(\hat{T}_\text{max}) + \frac{1}{2} \chi''(\hat{T}_\text{max}) (T - \hat{T}_\text{max})^2\big|_{T_C}
\end{equation}

where one can identify the terms $\chi(T_C) - \chi(\hat{T}_\text{max}) = \hat{\sigma}_{\hat{\chi}}(\hat{T}_\text{max})$ and $(T_C - \hat{T}_\text{max}) =\hat{\sigma}_{\hat{T_C}}$, and invert this relation to obtain an expression for the error on $\hat{T_C}$:

\begin{equation}
	\hat{\sigma}_{\hat{T_C}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \hat{\sigma}_{\hat{\chi}}(\hat{T}_\text{max})}{| \chi''(\hat{T}_C) |} }
\end{equation}

This is not an extremely solid statistical approach by any means, but it provides a reasonable estimate of the error on $\hat{T_C}$. As a common sense rule, this error is capped at half the temperature discretization step. The logic behind this decision is that the estimator $\hat{T_C}$ is very dependent on the position of the maximum sampled value, but the maximum sampled value can be at most one step away from the true maximum, which means that the error on $\hat{T_C}$ cannot be greater than half the step size. This is in practice rarely (if ever) used, as the error computed with the formula above is usually smaller than half the step size, and it exist just as a safety net to avoid problems with the code.

\br

The method described above produces an estimator $\hat{T}_C(N)$ that depends on the size of the lattice $N$. The real value of the critical temperature (for $N \to \infty$) is denoted by $T_C(\infty)$. The value of $\hat{T}_C(N)$ is expected to converge to the true critical temperature $T_C$ as $N \to \infty$, but for finite-size lattices it is not a good estimate of the critical temperature because of the systematic bias introduced by the finite size of the lattice.
There is a way to estimate $T_C(\infty)$ by performing a finite-size scaling analysis, that is, a way to estimate the value that $\hat{T}_C(N)$ approaches as $N \to \infty$.

The idea \cite{fisher1972} is to consider $\hat{T}_C(N)$ for different lattice sizes $N$, and then fit the data to a function of the form:

$$\hat{T}_C(N) = \hat{T}_C(\infty) + a N^{- \frac{1}{\nu}}$$

where $\hat{T}_C(\infty)$ is the estimated critical temperature in the thermodynamic limit, $a$ is a constant, and $\nu$ is the critical exponent.

The parameter $\nu$ is known to be $\nu = 1$ for the 2D Ising model (found by Onsager) and is estimated to be $\nu \approx 0.63$ for the 3D Ising model (this is a result of the renormalization group theory).


\section{1D Ising model}

The magnetization curves for the 1D case are presented in the following figure (\ref{fig:1D_magn}), which shows the magnetization $M(T)$ as a function of the temperature $T$ for different lattice sizes $N$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1.\textwidth]{../pics_and_anims/magn_graphs/magnetization_convergence_1D.pdf}
	\caption{Magnetization for the 1D Ising model.}
	\label{fig:1D_magn}
\end{figure}

This plot is consistent with the theoretical prediction presented in chapter 1. The values close to $1$ are simply an effect of finite-size corrections: the critical temperature would be exactly $0$ with infinite size for every positive $T$.

The reason why increasing the size of the system leads to the movement of the "step" towards $0$ is that the energy cost of breaking a streak of aligned spins is independent of the size of the system, which means that for bigger $N$ the probability of having a single big streak of aligned spins is lower, and for every streak of aligned-up spins there is a high probability a streak of a same length of aligned-down spins to exist, which leads to a null magnetization. Since the flipping of a spin is an effect of thermal fluctuations the magnetization is closer to zero for higher temperatures (this can be seen in the curves of the smaller lattices: they don't drop instantly but smoothly curve towards zero), but this effect is negligible for bigger lattices, where the magnetization drops to zero faster.

Trying to extract an approximation for the critical temperature $T_C$ from these data would be meaningless, as the expected behaviour is shown in the graphs, and an exact convergence is not achievable with a finite-size systems.

\section{2D Ising model}

The two dimensional Ising model presents an aesthetically pleasing result, as the convergence to the analytical solution is clearly visible. In figure \ref{fig:2D_magn} the magnetization $M(T)$ is shown for different lattice sizes $N$ along Onsager's line.

The theoretical value (explained in chapter 1) for the critical temperature of the 2D Ising model is $T_C \approx 2.269$.

The estimation of $T_C(L)$ and is shown in figure \ref{fig:2D_chi} for a 100x100 lattice.

The peak of $\chi(T)$ corresponds to the value of $T_C(N) = 2.31 \pm 0.04$, which is reasonable. More specifically, it is slightly higher than the accepted value, which is consistent with the expected relation $T_C(N) > T_C(\infty) \quad \forall \; N$.

The extrapolated value for the critical temperature in the thermodynamic limit is $T_C(\infty) = 2.283 \pm 0.015$, which is agreement with the accepted value $T_C \approx 2.269$ within one standard deviation.

\section{3D Ising model}

Results for the 3D Ising model are similar to the 2D case. Only, this time, in figure \ref{fig:3D_magn} the magnetization $M(T)$ is shown along the power law discussed in chapter 1.

The extracted value from the simulation is $T_C(L) = 4.50 \pm 0.08$, as shown in figure \ref{fig:3D_chi}, which is in agreement with the accepted value within one standard deviation.

	\caption{Example estimation of the critical temperature (3D case with 30x30x30 lattice).}
	\label{fig:3D_chi}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1.\textwidth]{../pics_and_anims/true_tc_extraction_3D.pdf}
	\caption{Estimation of the critical temperature (3D case) with proper finite-size scaling analysis.}
	\label{fig:3D_fss}
\end{figure}

Again, the finer method of analysis is to perform a finite-size scaling analysis, which is shown in figure \ref{fig:3D_fss}, yielding the estimate $T_C(\infty) = 4.5115 \pm 0.05$. This is also in agreement with the accepted value $T_C \approx 4.5115$ within one standard deviation.

It is however worth noting that the error in the 3D case is significantly higher than in the 2D case. This is a direct consequence of the fact that the error on $\chi$ described in the introduction section scales like $\frac{N^d}{\sqrt{n_{\text{ind}}}}$ where $n_{\text{ind}}$ is the number of (independent) samples in the chain after decorrelation. This means that not only is the error intrinsically larger for a larger system, but it also depends on the number of independent samples. In the context of these simulations $n_{\text{ind}}$ is not equal for the 2D and 3D cases, but it depends on $\tau_{\text{int}}$, which in turns depends on the size $N$ of the system (a system with a larger number of sites requires more steps to forget the initial configuration).

All the simulations were run for (roughly) the same number of steps, but the number of independent samples is different due to the longer decorrelation time in the 3D case. This can be appreciated by looking at the size of a sweep (which is $N^d$), which is a good first approximation of the decorrelation time $\tau_{\text{int}}$. That means that also $n$ depends on $N^d$, which clearly scales much faster for $d = 3$.

\section{General comments and Peierls argument}

But why does a finite critical temperature exist in 2D and 3D and not in 1D?

The answer can be understood in the context of the lattice approach by considering the energetic and entropic cost of flipping a single spin. This is the essence of the Peierls argument for spontaneous magnetization for 2D and 3D systems (1936).

Let's again consider the Helmholtz free energy $F = E - TS$, where $E$ is the internal energy, $T$ is the temperature, and $S$ is the entropy. If a system is in thermal equilibrium (like our configurations of Ising models), the free energy is minimized by definition.

Consider a long 1D lattice, and fix the spin at the boundary sites (let's say $+1$). Now, let's consider the effect of inserting a domain wall (a number of contiguous streak of aligned spins) of length $L$ with opposite spins in the middle of the lattice, say, at the origin. This operation has an energetic and an entropic cost.

Since the domain wall creates two boundaries, that is, two couples of adjacent, opposite spins, the energetic cost of this operation is $\Delta E = 4J$ where $J$ is the coupling constant of the Ising model. Note that this is true regardless of the length of the domain wall.

To compute the entropic cost, one can consider that the domain wall can be placed in $L$ different positions that include the origin along the lattice, which means that the entropy of the system increases by $\Delta S = k \ln(L)$, where $L$ is the count of possible configurations.

This means that the free energy of the system after this operation is:

\begin{equation}
	\Delta F = \Delta E - T \Delta S = 4J - T k \ln(L)
\end{equation}

which implies that for sufficiently large $L$ the free energy of the system can be reduced. It can be concluded that thermal fluctuations can spontaneously insert domain walls at arbitrarily small temperatures, leading to disorder and no net magnetization. The only caveat is that this argument is valid only for sufficiently long lattices, which explains why the drop in the magnetization curves in figure \ref{fig:1D_magn} moves toward zero increasing $N$: for low enough temperatures in a rather small lattice there are not enough spins to form a domain wall long enough to compensate for the energetic cost.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=.1\textwidth]{../pics_and_anims/ising_1D_N5_final.pdf}
	\caption{Short 1D lattice at any temperature $T > 0$.}
	\label{fig:1D_coarse}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1.\textwidth]{../pics_and_anims/ising_1D_N50_final.pdf}
	\caption{Long 1D lattice at any temperature $T > 0$.}
	\label{fig:1D_fine}
\end{figure}

This logic does not hold for higher dimensions.

For example, let's consider a 2D lattice with fixed spins at the boundary (again, let's say $+1$). Now, a domain wall in 2D is a region enclosed by a loop of sites with opposite spins. The energetic cost of establishing this wall is no longer independent of the size of the wall, but it is proportional to its length $L$, which means that $\Delta E = 2 J L$. Computing the entropic cost is more complicated, but it can be shown that the number of possible configurations of a domain wall of length $L$ is bounded by $c^L$ where $c$ is a constant greater than $\sqrt{2}$, which means that $\Delta S \sim k L \ln(c)$. This means that the free energy of the system after this operation is:

\begin{equation}
	\Delta F = \Delta E - T \Delta S = 2 J L - T k L \ln(c) = L (2J - T k \ln(c))
\end{equation}

from which it can be concluded that for $T < \frac{2J}{k \ln(c)}$ the free energy of the system can be reduced by removing domain walls, which leads to a net magnetization. Conversly, for $T > \frac{2J}{k \ln(c)}$, the disorder dominates and the magnetization vanishes. This is the essence of the Peierls argument, which shows that a finite critical temperature exists in 2D and 3D systems.
