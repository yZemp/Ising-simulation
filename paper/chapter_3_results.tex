\chapter{Results}

\section{Introduction}

After processing the data generated by the simulations to obtain the magnetizations, the critical parameter $T_C$ can be estimated.

As stated in chapter 1, while the drop in the magnetization curve suggests the temperature point at which the phase transition occurs, the numerically robust method to estimate this parameter is to look at $\chi$. In the following sections this method will be put into practice in two different ways.

The most simple and direct method to estimate the critical temperature is to take the maximum of $\chi$, $\chi$ is computed directly from the magnetization data using the result of the fluctuation-dissipation theorem, which states that the susceptibility can be computed from the variance of the magnetization as a function of temperature:

\begin{equation}
	\hat{\chi} = \frac{N^d}{T} \left( \langle M^2 \rangle - \langle |M| \rangle^2 \right)
\end{equation}

where the last term is the square of the average of the absolute value of the magnetization, which is used to avoid the cancellation of positive and negative values in the average.

Errors on the values of $\chi$ are computed using a the jackknife method for variance estimation \cite{jackknife-resampling, efron1982}, which is a resampling technique that allows to estimate the variance of a statistic by systematically leaving out one observation at a time from the sample set and calculating the statistic on the reduced sample. The estimator used is the following:

\begin{equation}
	\hat{\sigma}_{\hat{\chi}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{(n - 1) n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\chi_i - \bar{\chi})^2}
\end{equation}

where $n$ is the number of data points, $\chi_i$ is the value of $\chi$ computed on the $i$-th jackknife sample (that is, the original dataset with the $i$-th observation removed), and $\bar{\chi}$ is the mean of the $\chi_i$ values.

Given the discrete nature of the data in the temperature space, in order to better estimate the maximum of $\chi$ its maximum sampled value and the two nearest data points will be interpolated with a cubic spline. The maximum of the interpolated function will be taken as the estimate of the critical temperature $T_C$.

For a quick estimation of the error on $\hat{T_C}$ a simple method is to use the simple rule:

\begin{equation}
	\hat{\sigma}_{\hat{T_C}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \hat{\sigma}_{\hat{\chi}}(\hat{T}_\text{max})}{| \chi''(\hat{T}_C) |} }
\end{equation}

that can be derived by the Taylor expansion of $\chi(T)$ around the maximum point $\hat{T}_\text{max}$.

As a common sense rule, this error is capped at half the temperature discretization step. The logic behind this decision is that the estimator $\hat{T_C}$ is very dependent on the position of the maximum sampled value, but the maximum sampled value can be at most one step away from the true maximum, which means that the error on $\hat{T_C}$ cannot be greater than half the step size. This is in practice rarely (if ever) used, as the error computed with the formula above is usually smaller than half the step size, and it exist just as a safety net to avoid problems with the code.

\br

A better estimate of the critical temperature can be obtained by performing a finite-size scaling analysis, which allows to extrapolate a value for the critical temperature in the limit of infinite system size.

The idea \cite{fisher1972} [TODO: fix citation] is to extract the value of $T_C(N)$ for different lattice sizes $N$, and then fit the data to a function of the form:

$$T_C(N) = T_C(\infty) + a N^{- \frac{1}{\nu}}$$

where $T_C(\infty)$ is the critical temperature in the thermodynamic limit, $a$ is a constant, and $\nu$ is the critical exponent.

The parameter $\nu$ is known to be $\nu = 1$ for the 2D Ising model (found by Onsager) and is estimated to be $\nu \approx 0.63$ for the 3D Ising model (this is a result of the renormalization group theory).


\section{1D Ising model}

The magnetization curves for the 1D case are presented in the following figure (\ref{fig:1D_magn}), which shows the magnetization $M(T)$ as a function of the temperature $T$ for different lattice sizes $N$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1.\textwidth]{../pics_and_anims/magn_graphs/magnetization_convergence_1D.pdf}
	\caption{Magnetization for the 1D Ising model.}
	\label{fig:1D_magn}
\end{figure}

This plot is consistent with the theoretical prediction presented in chapter 1. The values close to $1$ are simply an effect of finite-size corrections: the critical temperature would be exactly $0$ with infinite size for every positive $T$.

The reason why increasing the size of the system leads to the movement of the "step" towards $0$ is that the energy cost of breaking a streak of aligned spins is independent of the size of the system, which means that for bigger $N$ the probability of having a single big streak of aligned spins is lower, and for every streak of aligned-up spins there is a high probability a streak of a same length of aligned-down spins to exist, which leads to a null magnetization. Since the flipping of a spin is an effect of thermal fluctuations the magnetization is closer to zero for higher temperatures (this can be seen in the curves of the smaller lattices: they don't drop instantly but smoothly curve towards zero), but this effect is negligible for bigger lattices, where the magnetization drops to zero faster.

Trying to extract an approximation for the critical temperature $T_C$ from these data would be meaningless, as the expected behaviour is shown in the graphs, and an exact convergence is not achievable with a finite-size systems.

\section{2D Ising model}

The two dimensional Ising model presents an aesthetically pleasing result, as the convergence to the analytical solution is clearly visible. In figure \ref{fig:2D_magn} the magnetization $M(T)$ is shown for different lattice sizes $N$ along Onsager's line.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1.\textwidth]{../pics_and_anims/magn_graphs/magnetization_convergence_2D.pdf}
	\caption{Convergence of magnetization for the 2D Ising model.}
	\label{fig:2D_magn}
\end{figure}

The accepted value for the critical temperature of the 2D Ising model is $T_C \approx 2.269$. For a finite lattice the  value $T_C(L)$ is an approximation for the critical temperature, and it is expected to converge to the accepted value as $L \to \infty$.

The estimation of $T_C(L)$ and is shown in figure \ref{fig:2D_chi} for a 100x100 lattice..

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1.\textwidth]{../pics_and_anims/tc_extraction_100_2.pdf}
	\caption{Example estimation of the critical temperature (2D case with 100x100 lattice).}
	\label{fig:2D_chi}
\end{figure}

The peak of $\chi(T)$ corresponds to the value of $T_C(L) = 2.31 \pm 0.04$, which is reasonable. More specifically, it is slightly higher than the accepted value, which is consistent with the expected relation $T_C(L) > T_C(\infty) \quad \forall \; L$.

As discussed in the introduction, a better estimate of the critical temperature can be obtained by performing a finite-size scaling analysis. The results of this analysis are shown in figure \ref{fig:2D_fss}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1.\textwidth]{../pics_and_anims/true_tc_extraction_2D.pdf}
	\caption{Estimation of the critical temperature (2D case) with proper finite-size scaling analysis.}
	\label{fig:2D_fss}
\end{figure}

The extrapolated value for the critical temperature in the thermodynamic limit is $T_C(\infty) = 2.283 \pm 0.015$, which is agreement with the accepted value $T_C \approx 2.269$ within one standard deviation.

\section{3D Ising model}

Results for the 3D Ising model are similar to the 2D case. Only, this time, in figure \ref{fig:3D_magn} the magnetization $M(T)$ is shown along the power law discussed in chapter 1.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1.\textwidth]{../pics_and_anims/magn_graphs/magnetization_convergence_3D.pdf}
	\caption{Convergence of magnetization for the 3D Ising model.}
	\label{fig:3D_magn}
\end{figure}

The analysis is equivalent to the 2D case. The accepted value for the critical temperature of the 3D Ising model is $T_C \approx 4.5115$, while the extracted value from the simulation is $T_C(L) = 4.50 \pm 0.08$, as shown in figure \ref{fig:3D_chi}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1.\textwidth]{../pics_and_anims/tc_extraction_30_3.pdf}
	\caption{Example estimation of the critical temperature (3D case with 30x30x30 lattice).}
	\label{fig:3D_chi}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1.\textwidth]{../pics_and_anims/true_tc_extraction_3D.pdf}
	\caption{Estimation of the critical temperature (3D case) with proper finite-size scaling analysis.}
	\label{fig:3D_fss}
\end{figure}

Again, the finer method of analysis is to perform a finite-size scaling analysis, which is shown in figure \ref{fig:3D_fss}, yielding the estimate $T_C(\infty) = 4.5115 \pm 0.05$. This is also in agreement with the accepted value $T_C \approx 4.5115$ within one standard deviation.

It is however worth noting that the error in the 3D case is significantly higher than in the 2D case. This is a direct consequence of the fact that the (jackknife) error on $\chi$ described in the introduction scales like $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{\text{ind}}}}$ where $n_{\text{ind}}$ is the number of (independent) samples in the chain after decorrelation. In the context of these simulations $n_{\text{ind}}$ is not equal for the 2D and 3D cases, but it depends on $\tau_{\text{int}}$, which in turns depends on the size $N$ of the system (a system with a larger number of sites requires more steps to forget the initial configuration).

Since the computational cost of simulating the 3D Ising model is much higher than the 2D case, the biggest lattice size that can be simulated is smaller ($N_{\text{max}}^{\text{2D}} = 100$ and $N_{\text{max}}^{\text{3D}} = 30$ in this project), which means that the number of independent samples is lower, which means that the error on the estimate of $T_C$ is higher.

\section{General comments and Peierls argument}

[

The equivalent of a streak of aligned spins in 2D is a cluster of aligned spins, and the equivalent of a single spin flip to break the streak is the flipping of the spins on the border of the cluster. The energy needed to flip a single spin is still independent of the size of the system, but the size of the perimeter of a cluster is not, and this introduces a size dependence in the probability of having a cluster of aligned spins, which is what leads to a threshold behavior.]

But why does a finite critical temperature exist in 2D and 3D and not in 1D?

The answer can be understood in the context of the lattice approach by thinking about the energy cost of flipping a single spin: this operation depends only on the nearest neighbours of the spin, which means that if $d$ is fixed, the flip energy cost does not change with $N$.

In general, the probability of having a streak of aligned spins is high (because that is the most stable configuration of the system). Now, let's consider a 1D lattice of very coarse size (think $N = 5$). In this case, even at relatively high temperatures, a configuration of two different streaks of aligned spins is improbable, because it is not energetically favorable. This implies a non zero magnetization even at non-zero temperatures like can be seen in figure \ref{fig:1D_coarse}, where a white or black square is an up or down spin.

This changes with finer lattices (think $N = 100$). In this case, the probability of having a configuration with two different streaks of aligned spins is much higher, because \textit{multiple long streaks of aligned spins are permitted}, and globally the magnetization averages out to zero. The key takeaway is that there does not exist a threshold temperature for this phenomenon, and the magnetization is always zero for sufficiently large $N$. This logic does not hold for higher dimensions.

In 2D, the equivalent of a streak is a cluster of aligned spins, and the equivalent of a single spin flip to break the streak is the flipping of the spins on the border of the cluster. Since the border of the cluster does actually scale with the size of the system, the probability of having a cluster of aligned spins is size dependent (because the energy cost of flipping the boundary of the cluster is), which means that there exists a minimum thermal excitation needed to break the cluster. The temperature at which this happens is the critical temperature. The same argument can be made about the 3D case, where the border of a cluster is a surface, which scales with the size of the system.
